Four public datasets and one stated guess. Two contact diary surveys, POLYMOD and France 2012, tell us how many new people we meet. The UN World Population Prospects tell us how big the pool is. HCMST, a Stanford survey, tells us who actually finds a partner. The guess is the chance that two soul mates recognize each other.

PyMC does the correcting. Every number tonight comes with a range of plausible values.

Keep this light. Prior, data, posterior, with the weather analogy. PyMC draws thousands of plausible answers and keeps them in proportion to how well they explain the data. That is why every number we show comes with an interval.

Before touching real data, the model has to find a truth we planted ourselves, here in four made up countries. Pencil is the truth, ink is the posterior with its 94% interval. This is also the first test in the repo. If it fails, nothing after it can be trusted.

POLYMOD, 8 European countries in 2005 and 2006, and a French survey from 2012. Each person listed everyone they met on a day, and how often they usually meet them. A contact counts after at least three words face to face, or a touch like a handshake. So strangers you only glance at are not in the data. We count the contacts marked as first time. Weekend days bring about 1.2 times as many.
field notes, entry no. 7

Only people your age count.

Your soul mate was born within 5 years of you. So were only 1 in 4 of the new people you meet.

this one fix changed everything.
This is the correction that changed the headline. The pool only holds people within 5 years of your age. But the diaries count new people of every age, and only 24.9% of them are that close in age. France gives 25.2%, almost the same. So we count only those.

Each country gets its own level, drawn from a shared distribution. That is the hierarchical part of the model. Italy and the Netherlands are the most talkative. Germany is far below the rest. Be honest here. Four countries, Germany among them, asked for an estimate of work contacts instead of a list. So part of this gap may come from the survey.

field notes, entry no. 11

The only numbernobody measured.

"I am 90% sure we would both know, with a chance between 0.3 and 0.9."

PreliZ turns the sentence into the flattest curve that fits it.

This is the honest part. p_recog is the chance that you both realize it at some point after you have met and talked. Nobody can measure it, so I state my guess as a sentence and let PreliZ maxent turn it into a prior, Beta(3.61, 2.47). Many curves put 90% between 0.3 and 0.9. Maximum entropy picks the flattest one, so I add no extra certainty. Ask the audience for their own bounds. The demo lets you type them in.
field notes, entry no. 12
1 in 325,800

the chance to find your one soul mate, somewhere on Earth.

94% interval: 1 in 1,500,000 to 1 in 115,000

Still, about 4,100 people of your generation do find theirs.

P_find is 1 minus (1 minus p_recog over M) to the power of the number of people you talk to. The posterior mean is about 3 in a million. The warmer reading is true as well: at these odds, about 4,100 of the 1.33 billion people of your generation find their one soul mate. It is rare, and it happens to someone.

How Couples Meet and Stay Together, 2017, US

Here the story turns. If each of us had exactly one soul mate, almost nobody would ever find a partner. HCMST, a survey by Rosenfeld and colleagues at Stanford, says 96% of people between 18 and 60 have had one. 2,520 respondents are in that age range.
field notes, entry no. 15

Some of us talk tomany strangers. Some to few.

Give every person their own number of contacts, and the model fits the young.

At ages 18 to 24 the data says 81% and the model 78%. Before, it said 68%.

Two partner models. In the simple one, each person you talk to is a possible partner with chance q, and everyone meets the same number of people. It misses the youngest by 13 points. In the better one, each person has their own contact rate, with a spread k of about 2.95. The French diaries, where people wrote down two days, show that such differences exist. Leave one out cross validation prefers this model by 10 points, with a standard error of 6. That is moderate evidence, and I say so.

5,783 dots: the people your age you will talk to. Rust: the 14 who could be the one.

This is the twist, and the thread splits. Of the roughly 5,783 people your own age you will talk to by 60, about 14 could become a partner. The interval is 10 to 19. One in 424, with an interval from 1 in 302 to 1 in 554. The model leaves fewer than 1 in 100 people without a partner by 60.
field notes, entry no. 17

In a room built for it:

1 in 6

Four minutes of talking. In 1 of 6 dates, both said yes.

Columbia speed dating study: 4,189 dates, 551 students. Fisman, Iyengar, Kamenica and Simonson, 2006.

students who signed up for dating. not the street.
A cross check from a very different setting, and it is not in the model. In the Columbia speed dating study, both people said yes after 16.5% of 4,189 four minute dates. 82% of the students got at least one match in an evening. These are students who signed up to date. Among all the people your age you talk to, it is 1 in 424. Where you talk to people matters as much as how many.
